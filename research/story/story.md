## 1 · Why now ✅

**Headline:** AI makes smart pricing possible for a store with no pricing team.

**Page text:**
Big retailers price with a team. Analysts, software that costs six figures a year, someone watching what sells and what doesn't. A small furniture store has none of that. It has the owner.

That used to mean the owner priced by feel, because there was no other way. Now there is. AI can take what the store already knows, what an item cost, how long it's been on the floor, whether it's selling, and turn it into a price and a floor. One owner can do what used to take a team.

Small businesses are already moving. In 2023, 23% of them used generative AI. By 2025 it was 58%. And the stores that don't move are disappearing. The US had 23,513 furniture stores in 2013 and 21,964 in 2023.

**Slide line:** Pricing used to need a team. Now it needs AI and the data the store already has.

**Spoken line:** Big retailers have a pricing team. A small furniture store has the owner. AI changes that.

**Sources:** generative-AI use 23% → 40% → 58%: US Chamber of Commerce / Teneo, Empowering Small Business 2025 (research/08, F-inflect-11). Enterprise price-optimization software around $100,000 a year: Pricefx estimate (research/03). Store counts: Census County Business Patterns 2013 and 2023, NAICS 442110 (research/08, F-inflect-07).

## 3 · Problem ✅

**Headline:** Pricing a furniture floor is a full-time job, and the owner doesn't have the time.

**Page text:**
Setting a price means weighing a lot at once. What the item really cost once freight's in. What's trending. What colors move in this store and which ones sit. Which brands hold their price. Most owners don't have time for that, so they multiply the cost by about two and never touch it again.

Then the price has to survive the sales floor. Salespeople get paid on the sale, not the margin, so a discount barely costs them. On a $2,000 sofa, 10% off costs the salesperson $12 and the store $200. Delivery gets thrown in for free. Some deals end up below cost.

When there's a sale, it's worse. Someone has to walk the floor, look at each piece, work out a new price, and retag it by hand, one at a time.

And the stock that doesn't sell just sits. A typical piece is on the floor about four months. At Jazz's store, about 10% has been there more than nine months.

That all lands on the owner's time:
- **Phone calls.** Salespeople call the owner to ask if they can take an offer. Jazz's parents got those calls all weekend when they were away.
- **Pricing on weekends.** Weekends are when the store is busiest and when the owner is least likely to be there.
- **Customers leave while they wait.** While the salesperson is on the phone, the customer stands there and other customers wait. Some of them leave.

**In Jazz's words:**
> "We got this item for $50 but I wanna sell it for $75." That's the call. She wants at least $100, and there's no number written down anywhere to settle it.

> "10% of our floor is more than nine months old and I would like that to be zero."

**What a real sale looks like:** a real invoice from her store, with every customer and store detail blacked out (image: `research/09-owner-evidence/invoice-1291-redacted.jpeg`). Two pieces, $2,794 of furniture, $99 delivery and $129 assembly charged on this one. The form has a line for what the customer paid. It has no line for what the tag said, so nobody can see what was given away.

**Slide line:** Prices set by feel, retagged by hand, discounted below margin, and an owner on the phone all weekend.

**Spoken line:** Every discount turns into a phone call. Customers wait, the owner gets pulled away, and some sales just walk out the door.

**Sources:** "Multiply by about two": owner interview and Furniture Today keystone coverage (research/01, F-pricing-05–08). No published data links color to sell-through, so the page says "in this store" (research/01 gaps). $12 vs $200: Census ARTS 2022 51% gross margin and 4–8% commission (F-industry-07, F-industry-16). About 120 days on the floor: Census ARTS 2022 and Furniture Today (F-industry-10). Quotes, 10% over nine months, weekend calls: owner interview answers (jazz-interview.md). Invoice: research/09 (original kept private). Customer-side confirmation: the floor-model buyer whose store manager "was at a wedding" (Reddit, 2020, verified).

## 4 · Solution (draft 2)

**Headline:** One place to set every price, and a floor no one can sell below.

**Page text:**
PriceLine is a pricing system for the owner. Every item carries a price that can change: when it's been sitting too long, when it's selling fast, when something's trending. The owner sets the rules once, and every price follows them.

Every item also carries a minimum, the lowest the store will take. Salespeople check offers against it on their phone, with delivery or assembly already counted in. Above the minimum, yes. Below it, no, with two counteroffers they can say right there. Nobody sells below it, and nobody has to call the owner.

Sales get easy too. The owner sees every item in one place, sets the sale there, and gets a printed list of exactly which tags changed. No walking the floor with a pen.

**Slide line:** Every item gets a price that can change and a minimum no one sells below. Sales are set in one place.

**Spoken line:** PriceLine gives every item a price that keeps up with the store, and a minimum the salespeople can't go under.

**Sources:** pricing rules and thresholds from the owner interview (jazz-interview.md, PRODUCT.md). The hard floor matches the app after Round HF: there is no ask-the-owner path.

## 5 · Product: what it does (draft 2, check each line against the live app)

**Headline:** What it does, in plain words.

**Page text:**
- **Prices and minimums.** Every item, its tag price, its minimum, and why. Change a rule and everything updates.
- **Your floor, as a picture.** See the whole showroom and find any piece on it, colored by how long it's been sitting.
- **Sales and retags.** Set a sale in one place and print the list of tags that changed.
- **Invoice intake.** Drop in a vendor invoice and the new pieces show up priced.
- **Dashboard.** Margin given up by each salesperson, deals that didn't close and why, and how much of the floor is old.
- **Ask anything.** Type a question the way you'd say it, like "what's been sitting longest in the back room," and get the answer with a chart. The AI is built in, so there's nothing to set up.
- **Deal check** for salespeople, on their phone.
- **Coming next: ML pricing.** Prices that learn from the store's own sales. The app already shows how close each store's data is to being ready.

It's made for owners who don't have time to learn software. Setup asks a few questions and the owner can pick their language. Every number has a little explanation next to it, and there's a glossary for words like "margin" and "minimum." Small things, but they save time every day.

Built and running on Emergent. Plans start at $200 a month per store.

**Slide line:** Prices and minimums, the floor as a picture, sales in one place, a dashboard, and questions in plain language. Made for owners who don't have time for software.

## 8 · Competition (draft)

**Headline:** The pieces exist. Nobody has put them together for a small store.

**Page text:**
Small stores already have tools, they're just scattered. The register (Square, Shopify, Lightspeed) lets a manager type a code to approve a discount, but it doesn't know what the lowest price should be. There are add-ons that block discounts below a margin, but they only work for online stores. There's a $10-a-month app that lets an owner approve something remotely, but it's built for convenience stores and doesn't know furniture. Price-tracking tools watch competitors' websites, not your own floor. And some furniture stores still write a coded price on the back of the tag.

The only furniture software that sends a discount request to a manager's phone is STORIS, and it's sold to the biggest chains. It checks fixed rules; it doesn't work out a price for each piece.

Nobody gives a small store one place to set every price, a minimum the floor can't go under, and a way to see the whole showroom. That's what PriceLine is.

**Table (site and deck):**

| What's out there | What it does | What it doesn't |
|---|---|---|
| Square, Shopify POS, Lightspeed ($89–289 a month) | Manager code to approve a discount; cost in reports after the sale | No minimum per item, no live check on the floor |
| Profit Guard ($0–30 a month) | Blocks discounts below a margin | Online stores only |
| NRS Plus remote override ($9.95 a month), Odoo discount limits (~$50) | Approve from a phone; per-person discount caps | Built for other kinds of store; no prices worked out |
| Prisync, Omnia ($99–399+ a month) | Track competitors' online prices | Nothing for the showroom floor |
| Furniture Wizard | Prints coded cost on tags | The code is on paper, and customers know where to look |
| STORIS | Sends discount requests to a manager's phone | Enterprise, sold to the top 100 chains; fixed rules, no worked-out price |
| vAuto, CarGurus (car dealers, $2,000+ a month) | Pricing and approval for car lots | Cars only, priced for dealerships |

**Slide line:** The pieces are scattered across registers, add-ons and enterprise software. Nobody combines them for a small store.

**Sources:** research/03-existing-tools.md (23-tool matrix, vendor pages; STORIS page read directly), research/12 (car dealers), research/10 (pricing pages).

## 9 · Business model (draft)

**Headline:** One price per store a month, and four levels.

**Page text:**
Every store pays a one-time $1,000 setup fee on Floor, Gallery or Forecast, and it's covered by the 30-day refund. That covers getting their inventory in, setting up their rules, and showing the staff how to use it. After that it's monthly, per store:
- **Floor, $200.** Prices, minimums and the deal check.
- **Gallery, $250.** Adds the floor as a picture, invoice intake, the markdown plan and the retag list.
- **Forecast, $450.** Adds the pricing data, the "similar items" view, lost-sale tracking, the sales calendar, and ML pricing as it comes in.
- **Sync, talk to us.** Digital ink price tags on every piece that update when a price changes. From $5,000 setup, plus tags at cost.

Billed from day one. If it isn't right for the store, they get a full refund within 30 days. A second store is $135 a month.

**Slide line:** $1,000 setup, then $200, $250 or $450 a month per store; digital tags on request. Full refund in 30 days.

**Sources:** live pricing page after Rounds T and T2; research/10 (pricing pages), research/15 (tag costs).

**Headline:** The more a store uses it, the smarter its prices get.

**Page text:**
Every deal, every sale that didn't close, every week an item sits is data. Once a store has enough of it, PriceLine moves from prices set by rules to prices that learn from what actually sells in that store. The app already shows each store how close it is.

Then we take it to other stores that haggle:

## 12 · Risks (draft)

**Headline:** What could go wrong, and what we're doing about it.

**Page text:**
- **Owners don't want new software.** It's one more thing to learn, and a lot of these owners are busy and not on computers much. So setup is done with them, it's in their language, and the first thing they see is their own store priced. We earn the next step.
- **Salespeople might not like it on the floor.** Checking a phone in front of a customer has to feel natural. We'll watch real salespeople use it and change how it looks and works until it does.
- **Someone bigger builds it.** A register company or an AI lab could. Our answer is to get there first with real stores: distribution through people the owners already trust, and the store's own sales data, which gets more useful the longer they use it.

The answer to all three is the same: go store by store, start with owners who already trust the person introducing us, and build what they actually use.

**Slide line:** Adoption, the floor experience, and bigger players. We answer all three by going store by store through people owners trust.
